Drum Grip

How you hold the sticks affects control, power, and comfort. Learn the main types and when to use them.

The different grips

  • Matched grip — Both hands the same. Includes:
    • French — Thumbs up, palms facing each other; more finger control.
    • German — Palms down; more wrist, good for power.
    • American — Hands at about 45°; balance of wrist and fingers.
  • Traditional grip — Left hand like a handshake, stick in the webbing; right hand matched. Common in jazz and marching.
Most common

Matched Grip

Both hands hold the sticks the same way — palms down or at an angle. Great for rock, pop, and most styles. Variations: French (palms in, finger control), German (palms down, wrist power), American (45° angle, mix of both).

Traditional Grip

The left hand holds the stick like a handshake, stick in the webbing; the right is similar to matched. Comes from military side drums. Common in jazz, drum corps, and marching.

Sticking & paradiddles

R = right hand, L = left hand. Practice slowly with a metronome, then build speed.

Single strokes
R L R L R L R L

Alternating; foundation of most grooves.

Single paradiddle
R L R R L R L L

Two singles, then a double per hand.

Double paradiddle
R L R R L L R L R R L L

Two doubles per hand; 12 notes.

Triple paradiddle
R L R R L R R L R L L R L L

Three strokes per hand in the double; 16 notes.

Paradiddle-diddle
R L R R L L

Single paradiddle plus a diddle at the end; 6 notes.

Inverted paradiddle
R L L R L R R L

Doubles in the middle; great for accents and fills.

Double stroke roll
R R L L R R L L

Two strokes per hand; essential for rolls and buzz.

Practice

Short, daily sessions beat long, rare ones. Consistency is key.

Daily time

Even 10–15 minutes daily builds muscle memory faster than one long weekly session.

Slow first

Get it clean and even at a slow tempo before speeding up. Accuracy before speed.

Use the metronome

Repeat & rest

Repeat patterns in short bursts. Take breaks to avoid tension and fatigue.

Five Stroke Roll

R R L L R. Two doubles plus one accent; five notes. Alternates L L R R L. Common in fills and as a building block for longer rolls.

06
Six Stroke Roll

R L R R L L. Six-note pattern; great for fills and hybrid grooves. Often accented on the first note of each hand.

Watch
07
Seven Stroke Roll

R R L L R R L. Three doubles plus one accent; seven notes. Strong forward momentum — great for building to a crash or downbeat.

Watch
08
Multiple Bounce Roll (Buzz)

Press the stick into the head so each stroke produces multiple bounces. Creates a sustained, smooth roll; essential for orchestral and concert snare.

Watch

Advanced rolls

Longer stroke rolls (9–15 notes) for fills, solos, and extended patterns.

09
Nine Stroke Roll

R R L L R R L L R. Four doubles plus one accent; nine notes. Longer build — use in fills and for a bigger lead-in to the downbeat.

Watch
10
Ten Stroke Roll

R R L L R R L L R R L. Four doubles plus a single; ten notes. Same idea as five/seven/nine — control the doubles, place the accent.

Watch
11
Eleven Stroke Roll

R R L L R R L L R R L. Five doubles plus one accent; eleven notes. Same family as five, seven, nine — build control and even doubles.

Watch
12
Twelve Stroke Roll

R R L L R R L L R R L L. Six doubles; twelve notes. Even roll with no accent — great for building consistency and length.

13
Thirteen Stroke Roll

R R L L R R L L R R L L R. Six doubles plus one accent; thirteen notes. Longer build — use in fills and snare solos.

Watch
14
Fourteen Stroke Roll

R R L L R R L L R R L L R R. Seven doubles; fourteen notes. Extended even roll — bridge to the fifteen stroke roll.

15
Fifteen Stroke Roll

Seven doubles plus one accent (R R L L R R L L R R L L R R L). Extended roll for long crescendos and dramatic fills; same pattern, more notes.

Drumming Styles

Genres and approaches every drummer should know. Build your basics first, then explore these.

Rock

Steady backbeat (snare on 2 & 4), driving kick patterns. Classic rock, punk, indie.

View examples
Pop

Clean, simple grooves; often electronic or hybrid. Tight time and consistency.

View examples
Funk

Syncopated grooves, ghost notes, 16th-note hi-hat. Groove over flash.

View examples
R&B / Soul

Pocket and groove; shuffles and half-time feels. Busier hi-hat and snare work.

View examples
Gospel

Church and worship drumming; strong pocket, dynamics, gospel chops and fills. Overlaps with R&B and funk.

View examples
Jazz

Swing feel, ride-cymbal time, comping. Brushes and improvisation; traditional grip common.

View examples
Blues

Shuffles and slow grooves; simple, strong backbeat. Foundation for rock and R&B.

View examples
Latin

Brazilian (samba, bossa), Afro-Cuban (mambo, cha-cha, songo), reggaeton, cumbia. Clave-based.

Metal

Double kick, fast singles, blast beats, odd meters. Power and precision.

Hip-Hop

Boom-bap, trap; tight kick and snare. Often swung or quantized grooves.

Reggae

One-drop, steppers; offbeat emphasis. Space and groove over busy fills.

Country
